CTR & Engagement Opportunity Scoring for Search Discoverability

Author: Ayush Narkhede  ·  Lane: CTR / Engagement Opportunity Scoring  ·  Track: FlyRank ML Internship Capstone

1. Abstract

High-impression pages that systematically under-capture clicks represent a clear, actionable opportunity for metadata and content review. Using the pseudonymized FlyRank internship warehouse, we built a time-aware panel of content performance (May → June 2026), defined a binary opportunity label (visible pages whose realized CTR falls well below the high-impression median), and trained transparent baselines plus gradient-boosted and random-forest classifiers under a client-holdout split. The best model reached ROC-AUC 0.781 (Average Precision 0.547) against a rule-based baseline of 0.577, delivering ranked queues that an editor can act on tomorrow. Results are directional decision-support only; no causal claims about Google’s ranking systems are made.

2. Introduction / Problem Statement

Editorial and SEO teams face a prioritization problem: which of the many pages that already attract impressions are most likely to benefit from a title, meta-description, or content refresh? Simple CTR thresholds ignore position, historical CTR, content structure, and keyword characteristics. This work produces a ranked opportunity list with reason codes so that limited editorial capacity is spent where the expected lift is highest.

Decision supported: “Given current visibility, which pages should we review for metadata or content updates this cycle?”

3. Data

Release: FlyRank Internship Warehouse v20260703 (Hugging Face, gated, public-safe terms).

  • fact_content_daily_performance (daily grain, month-partitioned) — aggregated to content × month for May and June 2026.
  • dim_content — static content / keyword attributes (character counts, search volume, competition, backlinks, etc.).
  • Date window used: May 2026 (features) → June 2026 (label window). Only rows with GSC data available.
  • Filters: impressions ≥ 50–100 in the feature month to focus on visible pages; client history depth respected via holdout.

Excluded: raw query strings, client names/domains, URL hashes beyond internal joins, AI-referral sparsity features for the primary label (AI sessions remain exploratory only). No client-identifying information appears in any public artifact.

4. Methodology

Assumptions & Label

  • Unit of analysis: pseudonymized content item (page) within a client.
  • Label (binary opportunity): page has ≥ 200 impressions in June and CTR < 0.75 × median CTR of high-impression pages. Observed positive rate on the test clients ≈ 22.6 %.
  • Features drawn only from the prior month (May) + static dim_content attributes — no June metrics enter the feature matrix (leakage control).

Feature set (14)

impr_may, clicks_may, ctr_may, avg_pos_may, keyword_char_count, keyword_token_count, url_char_count, search_volume, competition, cpc, backlinks, category_count, char_count, word_count.

Baseline

Transparent rule score: (1 − ctr_may) × log1p(impr_may). Higher scores flag historically low-CTR / higher-impression pages. Evaluated on the identical client-holdout test set.

Models

  • Logistic Regression (balanced, standardized features)
  • Random Forest (80 trees, max depth 7, balanced)
  • Gradient Boosting (60 estimators, max depth 3)

Validation design

Client-level holdout (≈ 70 / 30 of unique clients). No content from a test client appears in training. This respects the unbalanced panel nature of the warehouse and avoids leakage across a client’s pages. Random seed fixed at 42 for reproducibility.

  1. ctr_may (0.46) — prior-month CTR dominates
  2. impr_may (0.25) — visibility volume
  3. clicks_may (0.09)
  4. avg_pos_may (0.06)

The strong weight on prior CTR is expected; the model still adds lift by combining it with position, volume, and structural features.

6. Limitations & Honest Framing

  • Results are observed / directional. They support prioritization, not guaranteed traffic lift.
  • No experimental design (A/B or causal) was run; external shocks (algorithm updates, competitor changes, seasonality) remain unobserved.
  • Label uses a single future month; longer windows or multi-class trend labels could be explored in follow-up work.
  • AI-referral sessions are sparse and were deliberately kept out of the primary label to avoid unstable binary classification on rare events.
  • Panel is unbalanced across clients; client-holdout mitigates but does not eliminate all heterogeneity.

Language throughout is decision-support language only. No claim is made that the model recovers Google’s ranking function.

7. Ranked Recommendations (Action Playbook)

Score every eligible page with the Gradient Boosting probability. Rank descending. Apply reason codes from the dominant features:

  1. Tier 1 — High opportunity (top ~10–15 % of scores)
    Action: prioritize title + meta-description rewrite; check intent match and first-screen content.
    Typical reason codes: low ctr_may + solid impr_may + mid/high position.
  2. Tier 2 — Medium opportunity
    Action: content freshness / depth review; consider FAQ or structured data additions; monitor 30-day CTR after minor edits.
  3. Tier 3 — Monitor / protect
    Action: no immediate rewrite; keep in regular refresh cadence; watch for sudden CTR or impression drops.

Deliverable for an editor: a ranked CSV (content hash, client hash, score, top-3 reason codes, current CTR & impressions) that can be joined back to internal page maps. The ranking is the decision-support product; the model itself stays offline.
